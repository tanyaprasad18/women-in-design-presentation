# Lack of Inclusion of Women in Product Design

## Working thesis

The gender data gap is not just about silence. These silences and gaps have consequences that affect women every day. The impact can appear minor, such as shivering in offices set to a male temperature norm or struggling to reach a top shelf set at a male height norm. At its most serious, it contributes to preventable injury, misdiagnosis, and death.

The gap is not generally malicious or deliberate. It is the product of a way of thinking that has existed for millennia: treating the male body and male experience as universal, while treating women as a niche or exception. When we design a world meant to work for everyone, women need to be in the room.

## Definitions and framing

- **Data is information.** Statistics are one kind of information, but so is human experience.
- **Sex-disaggregated data** separates results by sex rather than combining women into a male default dataset.
- The data gap is particularly acute for women of colour, disabled women, and working-class women. The issue is not only that data is not collected, but that it is not separated from male data.
- The male experience has come to be seen as universal; the female experience, despite representing half the global population, is treated as specialised or niche.
- A 2015 study of multiple language Wikipedias found that articles about women used words such as “woman”, “female”, or “lady”, while articles about men generally did not use “man”, “masculine”, or “gentleman”. Maleness went without saying.

## Transportation and car design

### Motion sickness

- Consider whether women’s greater propensity for motion sickness in cars may be worsened by cars designed around the male body.
- Researcher Thomas Stoffregen’s response: this is plausible. If stabilisation is at the wrong height, it could affect the experience; women’s differing body sway may matter.

### Crash safety

- Men are more likely to be involved in car crashes and therefore dominate the overall number of people seriously injured.
- However, when a woman is involved in a crash, she is reported to be **47% more likely to be seriously injured**, **71% more likely to be moderately injured**, and **17% more likely to die** than a man, even after controlling for height, weight, seat-belt use, and crash intensity.
- Women tend to sit farther forward while driving because, on average, they are shorter. They need to be closer to reach pedals and sit more upright to see over the dashboard.
- This is not treated as the “standard seating position”; women are therefore described as “out of position” drivers. Sitting this way increases the risk of internal injury in frontal collisions.
- The angle of women’s knees and hips while reaching pedals also leaves legs more vulnerable in a collision.

### Rear-end collisions and whiplash

- Women have less muscle in the neck and upper torso on average, making them more vulnerable to whiplash, by up to three times.
- Swedish research found that modern car seats can be too firm to protect women against whiplash. The seat back gives less for lighter bodies and can throw women forward faster than men.
- Cars have historically been designed and assessed using crash-test dummies based on the “average” male.

### Crash-test dummies

- Crash-test dummies were introduced in the 1950s and were based for decades on the fiftieth-percentile male.
- The most common dummy is described as 1.77 m tall and 76 kg, with male muscle-mass proportions and a male spinal column. It is substantially taller and heavier than the average woman.
- Researchers argued for the inclusion of a fiftieth-percentile female in regulatory testing in the early 1980s; this advice was ignored.
- The United States began using a female crash-test dummy in 2011, but the “female” dummy was essentially a scaled-down male dummy.
- Women are not scaled-down men: muscle distribution, bone density, vertebrae spacing, and body sway differ. These differences are material to injury outcomes.

### Seat belts and pregnancy

- Seat belts have not yet been properly designed for pregnant women.
- Research from 2004 recommended use of the standard seat belt, but an estimated 62% of third-trimester pregnant women do not fit the standard design.
- A three-point belt can ride up on women who carry low. A 1996 study found this could treble or quadruple force transmission to the abdomen compared with a belt worn below the uterus, increasing the risk of fetal injury.
- Standard seat belts can also fit non-pregnant women poorly. Some women position the belt differently to accommodate breasts, which can increase injury risk.

## Healthcare, medicine, and research

### The cost of ignoring female bodies

- In 1960, doctors began prescribing thalidomide to pregnant women for morning sickness. It was considered safe because developers “could not find a dose high enough to kill a rat”.
- The drug affected fetal development; manufacturers reportedly knew about this risk as early as 1959.
- Before it was withdrawn in 1962, more than 10,000 children worldwide were born with thalidomide-related disabilities.
- This illustrates the danger of assuming that evidence from an incomplete population is sufficient.

### Female animals excluded from research

- Women are around 70% more likely to experience depression than men, but animal studies of brain disorders have been reported as five times more likely to use male animals.
- A 2014 paper found that, among studies of female-prevalent diseases which specified sex, only 12% studied female animals.
- Nearly all pain studies have historically been conducted exclusively in male mice.

### Heart devices and trial representation

- A 2014 review of FDA trials for cardiac resynchronisation therapy devices with defibrillators (CRT-D) found women were about 20% of participants.
- Individual trials had too few women for sex-specific findings to reach statistical significance. When results were pooled and disaggregated by sex, an important difference emerged.
- The usual threshold for implantation was a heart electrical wave taking 150 milliseconds or more.
- The meta-analysis found that the threshold was around 20 milliseconds too high for women. Women with a measurement between 130 and 149 milliseconds experienced a reported 76% reduction in heart failure or death, and 76% reduction in death alone, from the device, but would not have qualified under the existing guideline.
- Treating male bodies as the default can make clinical thresholds unsafe for women.

### Drugs and sex-specific effects

- Some clot-busting drugs given after a heart attack can cause significant bleeding problems in women.
- Some drugs commonly prescribed for high blood pressure have been found to lower men’s heart-attack mortality while increasing cardiac-related deaths among women.
- Statins have mainly been tested in men. Research from Australia suggested that women taking higher doses may face increased diabetes risk.
- In 2000, the FDA required manufacturers to remove phenylpropanolamine from products because of a reported increased risk of bleeding in or around the brain in women, but not men.

### Diagnosis and women’s symptoms

- UK research has found women are **50% more likely** to be misdiagnosed after a heart attack, rising to almost **60%** for some types.
- Women may not experience the stereotypical “Hollywood heart attack” symptoms of chest and left-arm pain.
- Women, particularly younger women, may instead present with stomach pain, breathlessness, nausea, and fatigue, sometimes without chest pain.

### Endometriosis example

- Rachael managed severe pain and heavy periods with the pill for ten years before collapsing at a gig.
- A hospital sent her home with painkillers and a diagnosis of stress. After another collapse, she was admitted to gastroenterology, investigated for kidney stones and urinary problems, and repeatedly received negative test results.
- As tests came back negative, she felt clinicians increasingly believed the pain was “all in her head”. A consultant eventually told her there was nothing wrong with her.
- She was eventually diagnosed with endometriosis, in which womb tissue grows elsewhere in the body and can cause extreme pain and infertility.
- Average diagnosis time: approximately eight years in the UK and ten years in the US. There is currently no cure.

## PPE, occupational health, and women’s safety

### Central point

Ill-fitting personal protective equipment is not a comfort issue. It can expose women to hazards, create trip and entanglement risks, prevent an effective mask seal, and make it practically difficult or unsafe to use the equipment at work. This puts women’s lives and health at risk.

### Evidence and examples

- A 2009 Women’s Engineering Society survey found that 74% of PPE was designed for men.
- The Trades Union Congress cautioned that a “unisex” approach can cause significant problems. Differences in chest, hip, and thigh shape affect safety-harness straps.
- Dust, hazard, and eye masks often use a standard US male face shape. They may not fit most women, as well as many Black and minority ethnic men.
- Loose clothing and gloves can become caught in machinery; oversized boots can cause tripping.
- Women have smaller hands on average, yet equipment is repeatedly designed around the average male hand as if one-size-fits-men meant one-size-fits-all.

### Outdoor and emergency work

- UK coastguards are issued one-piece overalls to wear beneath other PPE, including foul-weather gear, life jackets, and climbing harnesses.
- A front double zip works for men, but women may have to remove all PPE and then the overalls to urinate, making it a “major operation”.
- A female climate scientist working in Alaska faced the same issue with male-designed overalls. She bought a rubber funnel intended to make urination easier and ended up urinating on herself.
- These examples show that PPE design can affect dignity, hydration, comfort, and whether someone can safely remain equipped for the job.

### Military equipment

- Failing to account for female bodies in military equipment can injure women as well as reduce performance.
- Women in the British Army have been found up to seven times more likely than men to experience musculoskeletal injuries, even with the same aerobic fitness and strength, and ten times more likely to suffer hip and pelvic stress fractures.
- It was not until 2011, 35 years after women entered US military academies, that uniforms designed for women’s hips and breasts were introduced.
- The redesign included repositioned knee pads for shorter legs and a crotch design allowing women to urinate without pulling down their trousers. Boots designed for typically narrower women’s feet and higher arches were still not included.

### Tools, equipment, and workspaces

- Occupational health data gaps are sometimes excused by the fact that men are more likely to die at work. This can hide hazards that are differently experienced by women.
- Virtually all tools and equipment on the US market have been described as designed for men, or for an “average” user heavily influenced by the average man.
- Consequences include tools that are too heavy or long; poorly balanced hand tools; grips and handles of the wrong size or position; and machinery that is difficult to control. Women’s hands are on average 0.8 inches shorter than men’s.
- Example: tractor pedals can be positioned too far from the seat.
- Wendy Davis of the Women’s Design Service questioned why a cement bag needs to be a size comfortable for a man to lift, or why a standard brick needs to be so wide that many women cannot grip it securely.
- An A1 architect’s portfolio fits under many men’s arms, but may be too wide for many women to carry in the same way.

### Tactile pilot vest

- The tactile situation awareness system (TSAS) is a vest for Air Force pilots with 32 vibrating sensors to help a pilot orient themselves in space.
- A review used male pronouns throughout while noting vibration is best detected on hairy, bony skin and is harder to detect on soft, fleshy areas.
- Women make up around 20% of the US Air Force. The interaction between breasts, chest anatomy, and sensor placement should be explicitly tested rather than assumed away.

## Everyday products and built environments

### Temperature, furniture, and buildings

- Standard office-temperature formulas were developed in the 1960s around the metabolic resting rate of an average 40-year-old, 70 kg man.
- A later study found young adult women performing light office work have a significantly lower metabolic rate than the standard values. The formula may overestimate women’s metabolic rate by up to 35%.
- This can make offices on average about five degrees too cold for women.
- Other examples: doors too heavy for the average woman to open easily; glass stairs and lobby floors that can expose people wearing skirts.
- Top shelves and other standard reach assumptions can be set around male height norms.

### Public toilets

- Equal floor space for male and female toilets is not necessarily equal access.
- Male bathrooms can accommodate more people per square foot because they include urinals as well as cubicles.
- Even with equal numbers of stalls, women can take up to 2.3 times as long to use a toilet.
- Women are more likely to accompany children, disabled people, and older people. Around 20-25% of women of childbearing age may be menstruating at any point and may need to change menstrual products.
- Public-toilet shortages are especially acute for women because men are more socially able to “go anywhere”, while women can be shamed for being seen urinating.
- Lack of access can lead women to avoid drinking and avoid using the toilet throughout the day, increasing dehydration and heat-illness risk.
- Potential case study to verify and develop: Swachh Bharat Mission failures.

### Phones, bags, clothing, and pockets

- Smartphone design increasingly prioritised large screens. The average smartphone is described as 5.5 inches, a poor fit for many women’s hands and often with limited or no adequate pockets in women’s clothing.
- Women may choose larger phones because they carry handbags, but the need for handbags is itself connected to clothing without functional pockets.
- Other product prompts: women’s bags, footwear, kitchens, and gym/workout equipment.

### Fitness equipment

- Treadmill calorie calculations are often based on the average male body and a default user weight of around 11 stone.
- Changing the weight setting does not fully correct the calculation because women’s average fat-to-muscle distribution and muscle-fibre ratios differ.
- Even at the same weight, men are estimated on average to burn 8% more calories than women; a male-calibrated treadmill does not account for this.

### Consumer technology

- Smartwatches can be too large for women’s wrists.
- Map apps may provide only the “fastest” route rather than accounting for a user’s need for the “safest” route.
- Voice-recognition tools can be male biased. A 2016 study by Rachael Tatman found Google speech recognition was 70% more likely to accurately recognise male speech than female speech, despite Google being described as the strongest system then available.
- Example: a woman whose Buick voice-controlled phone system would not understand her was told by the manufacturer it would not work for her and to get a man to set it up.
- Example: lowering the pitch of a woman’s voice allowed a Volvo system to recognise a request after repeated failures.
- Medical dictation research noted that women may have to work harder than men to make voice-recognition systems work.

## AI, algorithms, and digital design

- AI systems learn from historical data. When the data is biased or incomplete, systems can reproduce and amplify that bias.
- In an image dataset study, cooking images were over 33% more likely to show women, but algorithms trained on the set associated kitchens with women 68% of the time.
- The greater the original bias, the stronger the amplification effect. One system labelled a photo of a balding man in front of a stove as female: “kitchen” outweighed other signals.
- A researcher described a future in which a robot uncertain what someone is doing in the kitchen offers a man a beer and a woman help washing dishes.
- AI diagnostics are being introduced with too little acknowledgement of chronic gaps in women’s medical data. Machine learning could make diagnosis worse for women, potentially fatally.
- AI job recruitment is a topic to develop: automated hiring can encode historical occupational and gender bias.

## Design culture, representation, and participation

### Who is imagined as the default?

- Feminist Frequency’s E3 2016 analysis observed that “heroes are male by default”.
- The result of male-dominated culture is that male experience and perspective are treated as universal.
- Product design starts by deciding which problems deserve solving. That decision is shaped by perception and by who is present. Example question: what problem were NASA designers solving when they gave the space-navigation robot Valerie breasts?

### VR and safety by design

- VR is often created by male-led companies and can contain built-in male bias.
- VR gaming has a sexual-harassment problem that developers can fail to anticipate.
- In the multiplayer VR game *QuiVr*, author and gamer Jordan Belamire reported being sexually assaulted by another player.
- The designers responded proactively by expanding their “Personal Bubble” feature so other players’ hands disappeared when approaching the whole body, not only the face.
- They had anticipated someone blocking another player’s view, but not groping. This is a clear example of what becomes visible when women’s experiences are included in design.

### Women’s health products and investment

- When Janica Alvarez sought investment for Naya Health, a breast-pump start-up, some investors reportedly searched for the product and landed on a pornographic site, joked about it, were reluctant to touch the product, or called it disgusting.
- The example shows how male discomfort and ignorance can decide which women’s needs are treated as legitimate design problems.

### Pregnancy in the workplace

- When Sheryl Sandberg was pregnant while working at Google, severe swelling and morning sickness made its large car park difficult to cross.
- She asked Sergey Brin for pregnancy parking near the building. He agreed immediately and said he had never thought about it.
- Sandberg herself had not realised reserved parking was needed until she experienced the difficulty. Lived experience changes what needs designers and decision-makers can see.

## Urban design and public services

### Snow clearing in Karlskoga

- Karlskoga’s original snow-clearing schedule prioritised major traffic arteries, then pedestrian walkways and cycle paths.
- This affected men and women differently because travel patterns differ. In France, two-thirds of public-transport passengers are women; in Philadelphia and Chicago, the figures are reported as 64% and 62% respectively.
- Men globally are more likely to drive and, where a household owns a car, men tend to have greater access to it, including in Sweden.
- Men’s travel patterns are more likely to be a simple twice-daily commute. Women undertake 75% of the world’s unpaid care work, creating more complex journeys: school drop-offs, medical appointments for relatives, shopping, and work. This is known as “trip chaining”.
- The schedule was not intentionally designed to privilege men. The people who designed it knew their own journeys and did not think to consider other patterns. This is a gender data gap in perspective.

## Education, media, and gendered expectations

- Gender plus occupation in children: develop examples of how children learn which work is “for” women or men.
- **The Sexy Lamp Test:** A media-representation critique introduced by comic-book writer Kelly Sue DeConnick around 2010. The question is: if a female character were replaced by a “sexy lamp”, would the story meaningfully change? If not, she is present only as decoration, a sexualised prop, or a motivator for male characters rather than as a person with agency.
- This is not a formal academic study and it does not produce a numerical finding. Its value is as a quick diagnostic: it reveals when women are included visually but excluded from meaningful action, decision-making, expertise, and plot development.
- Relevance to design: representation shapes who is imagined as a user, expert, hero, or decision-maker. If women are routinely decorative or secondary in media, it becomes easier for designers and institutions to treat male needs as universal and women’s needs as niche.
- Advertising can reinforce gendered roles: cookers, washing machines, jewellery, and other products.
- School uniforms can restrict girls’ movement and activity.
- Claims that women choose low-paid work overlook whether realistic alternatives exist.
- Schools can teach “brilliance bias” to boys. In decades of “draw a scientist” research, children overwhelmingly drew men.
- A recent meta-analysis was celebrated because 28% of children now draw female scientists, up from 1% in the 1960s. This is progress but still below reality.
- In the UK, women outnumber men in numerous science degrees, including polymers (86% women), genetics (57%), and microbiology (56%).
- School textbooks and the “happy house” model may reinforce domestic gender roles.

## Pink tax

- Include a section on the “pink tax”: gendered pricing and the tendency for substantially similar goods marketed to women to cost more.
- Separate two issues in the talk: pricing discrimination and design/data exclusion. They can reinforce each other but are not the same claim.

## Possible talk structure

1. **Open with the premise:** data gaps are not neutral; they affect safety, health, time, money, and dignity.
2. **Make the invisible visible:** explain data, sex-disaggregation, and male-as-default thinking.
3. **Use car safety as the main product-design case study:** crash dummies, seating position, whiplash, and pregnancy.
4. **Raise the stakes with healthcare:** trial representation, diagnostic patterns, drugs, and endometriosis.
5. **Focus on PPE as a workplace safety case study:** ill-fitting masks, harnesses, gloves, boots, and one-piece overalls.
6. **Show daily-life examples:** office temperature, toilets, phones, tools, fitness equipment, and public transport.
7. **Look forward:** biased AI, recruitment, diagnostics, and VR.
8. **Close with an action:** collect sex-disaggregated data, test with diverse women, include women in decision-making, and design for real use rather than a presumed default user.
