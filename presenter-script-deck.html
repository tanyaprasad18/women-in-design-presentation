Title and audience game

Hello everyone. I’m Tanya Prasad, a Product Designer with IBM and HashiCorp. Before I begin, a quick game. Raise your hand if any of these feel familiar.

  • Raise your hand if you have ever gone to buy a phone and asked for a smaller size.
  • Raise your hand if you have ever carried a bag because your pockets were too small, fake or missing.
  • Raise your hand if you have ever worn something labelled “free size” and thought: clearly, it is not.
  • Raise your hand if you have ever been freezing in an office while somebody else said, “The temperature is perfect.”
  • Raise your hand if you sit cross-legged because it is more comfortable, or because you keep sliding out of the chair.
  • Raise your hand if you have ever been told a product is “unisex” when it was clearly designed around someone else.

We spend a surprising amount of time adapting ourselves to products, spaces and systems meant to work for everyone. Today: One Size Does Not Fit All.

Mobile phones

Start with a phone. Screens got bigger, and bigger became better. But the average smartphone is around 5.5 inches, not equally comfortable for every hand. I can get stronger, but I cannot make my hands bigger. This is ergonomics, not preference. Standard for whom?

Pockets

If the phone does not fit in your hand, put it in your pocket. Except women’s pockets often hold one receipt and a dream. Small, fake or absent pockets make bags a workaround, not simply a preference.

Toilet layout

The women’s toilet queue is a familiar design problem. Equal floor space is not equal access: men’s toilets fit urinals and cubicles in the same area. Women can take up to 2.3 times as long to use a toilet, with periods, children and caring responsibilities adding to the queue.

06

1:42-1:53

Parking

In 2014, Sheryl Sandberg was pregnant and working at Google. Walking across its huge car park became difficult, so she asked Sergey Brin for pregnancy parking. He agreed, saying he had never thought about it. Needs become visible when people with those needs are present.

Presenter notes

07

1:53-2:01

Glass staircases

Glass stairs may look beautiful in a render. But for someone in a skirt, they can make privacy optional. Design communicates who can move freely through a space.

Presenter notes

08

2:01-2:14

Office temperature

The office-temperature formula was developed in the 1960s around a forty-year-old, 70-kilogram man. It may overestimate women’s metabolic rate by 35 percent. If you are wrapped in a blanket in summer, you are living inside somebody else’s default setting.

Presenter notes

09

2:14-2:26

Automobile seating

Women are called “out of position” drivers because shorter drivers sit closer to the pedals and upright to see over the dashboard. But that only sounds wrong because the car was designed around a different body. This can increase injury risk and may contribute to motion sickness.

Presenter notes

10

2:26-2:38

Seatbelts

Research found 62 percent of third-trimester pregnant women do not fit the standard seatbelt design. The lap belt should sit low across the hips, beneath the abdomen. Poor fit should trigger redesign, not a warning label.

Presenter notes

11

2:38-2:58

Crash tests

Women are 73 percent more likely to be injured in a head-on collision and 17 percent more likely to be killed as drivers or front-seat passengers. The five-star test used a 5-foot-9, 171-pound male dummy, developed in 1978. The female dummy represents only the smallest 5 percent of US women. Women are not scaled-down men. THOR-05F, with 150-plus sensors, is expected by 2027 or 2028.

12

2:58-3:10

Sexy Lamp Test

Comic-book writer Kelly Sue DeConnick coined the Sexy Lamp Test: could a female character be replaced by a decorative lamp without changing the plot? If yes, she is a prop, not a person with agency. In 2016, Feminist Frequency said: heroes are male by default.

3:10-3:18

Bags and shoes

Accessories often compensate for exclusion. If clothing cannot carry what you need, you carry the workaround. Who was given practical pockets in the first place?

Presenter notes

3:18-3:29

AI and recruitment

Algorithms inherit their training data. Historical bias can become automated bias: recruitment tools can reward past exclusion, while image systems can amplify stereotypes.

Presenter notes

15

3:29-3:46

Voice, maps and VR

In 2016, University of Washington researcher Rachael Tatman found Google speech recognition was 70 percent more likely to accurately recognise male speech than female speech. Maps optimise for fastest, not always safest. After Jordan Belamire reported assault in the VR game QuiVr, developers redesigned its personal-bubble feature. Foreseeable, not edge-case, needs.

Presenter notes

16

3:46-3:57

Education

In 1960s “draw a scientist” studies, only 1 percent of children drew a woman. Today it is 28 percent, better but still not reality. In the UK, women outnumber men in polymers, genetics and microbiology degrees. Representation shapes what children imagine is possible.

Presenter notes

17

3:57-4:11

Heavy duty tools

Tools are often designed for men or an “average” user shaped by men: handles too large, equipment too heavy, pedals too far away. Women’s hands are, on average, 0.8 inches shorter. A brick or cement bag does not have to be its current size. “Standard” is a choice.

Presenter notes

18

4:11-4:21

Safety vests

“Unisex” does not guarantee fit. A vest designed around male proportions can gap, shift, restrict reach and create snag hazards. Fit-test with women doing the actual work.

Presenter notes

20

4:32-4:43

TSAS

The tactile situation-awareness system uses 32 vibrating sensors to orient pilots. Yet the research described a default user as “his” body, despite acknowledging vibration is detected differently across bodies. Pronouns can reveal assumptions.

Presenter notes

21

4:43-4:58

PPE

A 2009 Women’s Engineering Society survey found 74 percent of PPE was designed for men. Loose gloves can catch, oversized boots can trip, and male-pattern masks can leave gaps. PPE that does not fit is not protective equipment.

Presenter notes

22

4:58-5:15

Heart attacks and CRT-D

Women are about 50 percent more likely to be misdiagnosed after a heart attack because “Hollywood” chest-and-left-arm symptoms are treated as standard. Women may instead have nausea, fatigue, breathlessness or stomach pain. In 2014, CRT-D research found women were only about 20 percent of trial participants, yet could benefit at lower thresholds than guidelines set around men.

Presenter notes

23

5:15-5:26

Endometriosis

Endometriosis causes severe pain and can affect fertility, but diagnosis takes about eight years in the UK and ten in the US. Too often, women are sent through the wrong tests or told it is stress. Sometimes “nothing is wrong” is a data gap speaking.

Presenter notes

24

5:26-5:38

Breast pumps and investment

In 2013, Naya Health founder Janica Alvarez reported that investors refused to touch her breast pump or called it “disgusting.” The issue is not only who gets designed for, but who gets funded, believed and considered commercially valuable.

Presenter notes

25

5:38-5:50

Pink tax: product examples

The pink tax is not a government tax. It is gender-based price discrimination: near-identical goods marketed to women can cost more. Studies place the average difference at roughly 7 to 13 percent in Western markets, reaching 19.4 percent in some recent Indian assessments.

Presenter notes

26

5:50-6:00

Pink tax: more examples

Razors, toiletries, toys, clothing, dry cleaning and salon services all show the pattern. Compare unit price and function, not pink packaging. Same job, higher cost.

Presenter notes

27

6:00-6:25

Conclusion

Inclusive design does not mean making everything pink or purple. It can be black, grey or any colour. What matters is whether women can use it effortlessly. Good inclusive design means faster adoption, fewer usability mismatches, fewer complaints and fewer people becoming the product’s beta tester.

It does not need to announce inclusion. It simply works.

Presenter notes

28

6:25-7:40

Closing question

The gender gap is not generally malicious. It is often a gap in information and imagination. But when women of colour, disabled women and working-class women are missing from research, or data is not separated by sex, the gap grows wider.

The male experience becomes universal; women become “niche.” The response is practical: collect sex-disaggregated data, include diverse women in research and decision-making, and test products with the people who will actually use them.

So the question I leave with you is: who is missing from the data, the room and the design?
