5. Toilet Layout

1:27-1:42

Toilet Layout

Now lets think of another scenario, the movie screen has shown ‘Interval and you see a flood of women rushing towards the washroom. Raise your hands if you have stood in a long queue and almost missed making it back on time for the movie.

The women’s toilet queue is a familiar design problem. On the face of it, it may seem fair and equitable to accord male and female public toilets the same amount of 50/50 loor space – and historically, this is the way it has been done. However, the same floor areas dont carry the same number of relieving units. Suddenly equal floor space isn’t so equal.

But even if male and female toilets had an equal number of stalls, the issue wouldn’t be resolved, because women take up to 2.3 times as long as men to use the toilet.

Women are also more likely to be accompanied by children, as well as disabled and older people. Then there’s the 20-25% of women of childbearing age who may be on their period at any one time,and therefore needing to change a tampon or a sanitary pad. The consequence? Women refrain from relieving themselves at all during the day – aided by avoiding drinking liquids, which increased their risk of dehydration and urinary infections

9. Office Temperature

Office Temperature

The office-temperature formula was developed in the 1960s around a forty-year-old, 70-kilogram Caucasian man. The same temp may overestimate women’s metabolic rate by 35 percent simple, meaning that current offices are on average five degrees too cold for women. because our bodies are different. Which leads to the odd sight of female office workers wrapped up in blankets in summer while their male colleagues wander around in summer clothes.

10. Automobile Seating

Automobile Seating

Women tend to sit further forward than men when driving. This is because we are on average shorter. Our legs need to be closer to reach the pedals, and we need to sit more upright to see clearly over the dashboard. This is not, however, the ‘standard seating position’ and we are at greater risk of internal injury on collisions.

12. Crash Tests

2:48-3:08

Crash Tests

Women are also at higher risk in rear-end collisions. Women have less muscle on our necks and upper torso than men, which make us more vulnerable to whiplash (by up to three times), and car design has amplified this during a crash. The reason this has been allowed to happen is very simple:

From 1950s,crash test dummies were based around the fiftieth percentile male. The most commonly used dummy is 1.77 m tall and weighs 76 and the dummy also has male muscle-mass proportions and a male spinal column. It wasn’t until 2011 that the US started using a female crash-test dummy, and funny still, this female dummy is not really female. It is just a scaled-down male dummy.

22. PPE

4:53-5:08

PPE

A 2009 Women’s Engineering Society survey found 74% of PPE was designed for men. Loose gloves can get caught, oversized boots can trip, and masks can leave gaps. PPE that does not fit is not protective equipment. Gaps around the nose, cheeks, and chin allow airborne viral particles to leak through, significantly increasing infection risks. Additionally, full-body suits made it difficult for women to quickly use the restroom or manage menstruation during extended shifts . A major threat for women heatworkers during Covid.

23. Heart Attacks and CRT-D

5:08-5:25

Heart Attacks and CRT-D

Women are about 50 percent more likely to be misdiagnosed after a heart attack because “Cinamat ic” chest-and-left-arm symptoms are treated as standard. Women do not have the same symptoms and may instead have nausea, fatigue, breathlessness or stomach pain.

A cardiac resynchronisation therapy device(CRT-D) is used to correct a delay in your heart’s electrical signals.. if your heart takes 150 milliseconds or longer to complete a full electrical wave, you should might need to have one implanted. While the 150 milliseconds threshold worked for men, it was twenty milliseconds too high for women, Making women illegible for it according to ‘standard’.

26. Pink Tax: Product Examples

5:48-6:00

Pink Tax: Product Examples

The pink tax is not a government tax. It is gender-based price discrimination: near-identical goods marketed to women can cost more. Studies place the average difference at roughly 7 to 13 percent in Western markets, reaching 19.4 percent in some recent Indian assessments.

28. Conclusion

6:10-6:35

Conclusion

Inclusive design does not mean making something exclusively for women or making everything pink or purple. It can be black, grey or any colour. What matters is whether women can use it effortlessly, comfortably. Good inclusive design means faster adoption, fewer usability mismatches and fewer lives being at risk. Bad design decisions should not be the cause of risk for someones life.

29. Closing Question

6:35-7:40

Closing Question

The gender gap is not necessarily malicious or intentional. It is often a gap in information and imagination. The male experience becomes universal; women become “niche.” The solution is simple: collect sex-inclusive data, include diverse women in research and decision-making, and test products with the people who will actually use them.

So the question I leave with you is: who is missing from the data, the decision making room and hence the design?
